Joint Distributions

Gary Mitchell

October 30, 2019

LN 10 - When Two Outcomes Occur Together discusses joint distributions. A joint distribution occurs when we have values of two random variables that occur together. The lecture notes do a nice job of providing a joint probability overview. This tutorial focuses on showing you how to apply the concepts and calculations relating to joint distributions in R.

Let’s say that we want to determine the probability that the Mariners win 80 or fewer games and score more than 800 runs in a season, i.e. \(Pr\{wins \leq 80 \cap total \; runs \geq 800\}\). This is an example of a joint probability:

The probability of two random events occurring together.

If the two random variables (i.e. # of wins in season, # of runs in season) are independent, then we could calculate

\[Pr\{wins \leq 80 \cap total \; runs \geq 800\}=Pr\{wins \leq 80\} \cdot Pr\{runs \geq 800\}\]

However, if the variables are not independent, or if we do not know whether they are independent, then we would need to use a joint distribution.

Joint Distributions

So what is a joint distribution? In its simplest form, it is a set of outcomes for two random variables and the corresponding probabilities that the outcomes occur together. For example, the table below represents the joint probability distribution of Mariner runs and wins in a season. Using the Joint Distribution

Now that we have the joint distribution, we can find any joint probability we want. For example, to calculate \(Pr\{wins \leq 80 \cap total \; runs \geq 800\}\), we would do the following:

\[Pr\{wins \leq 80 \cap total \; runs \geq 800\} = \sum_{w \leq 80} \sum_{r \geq 800} Pr\{W \leq w \; and \;R \geq r\} \]

Calculating this in R is straightforward once we have our distribution table (saved as mdist in our code). Because we have constructed the joint distribution table such that each row is one joint outcome with its corresponding probability, we need only sum the probabilities for the events that satisfy our criteria (i.e. \(W \leq 80\) and \(R \geq 800\)):

\[Pr\{wins \leq 80 \cap total \; runs \geq 800\} = \sum_{i \in \{W \leq 80, R \geq 800\}} p_i \]

This is easily accomplished with the following R code which shows that the Mariner’s have a less than 5% chance of scoring more than 800 runs while racking up 80 or fewer wins.

sum(mdist$p[((mdist$W <=80) & (mdist$R>=800))])
## [1] 0.04878049

We can also use the joint distribution to calculate a marginal probability (i.e. the probability of runs scored or the probability of wins). For example, \(Pr\{wins \leq 80\}\) is calculated simply using:

sum(mdist$p[mdist$W <= 80])
## [1] 0.7073171

and \(Pr\{runs \geq 800\}\) is calculated with:

sum(mdist$p[mdist$R >= 800])
## [1] 0.1707317

That looks better! Now we’re ready to go. The first step in constructing the joint distribution is to count the occurrence frequencies of all possible outcomes (i.e. the pairs of runs and wins). The aggregate method is really useful here and will do most of the work for us. We’ll name the key columns RUNS and WINS. aggregate will name the results columns R and W (which will include the frequencies), so we initially need to name our key columns something else (we don’t want two columns with the same names). Then, we’ll rename the columns to more meaningful names:

mdist<-aggregate(mdat, by=list(RUNS=mdat$R, WINS=mdat$W), length)
colnames(mdist)<-c("R","W","freq","W.freq")

aggregate will extract the data for each unique pair of R and W, the by variables. Normally, we would use the sum function, or another summary statistic function, to calculate summary metrics for the subset. However, in this case, we only want to count the number of observations. In this way, we will calculate the frequency of occurrence for every pair of R and W. The values in the freq and W.freq columns will end up being identical since they are counts of the same observations, so we’ll drop the W.freq column.

mdist<-mdist[, c("R","W","freq")]

Next, we will calculate the probabilities which, since they are calculated for R/W pairs, will be joint probabilities. As we learned in the calculating probabilities from frequencies tutorial, we calculate a probability as follows:

\[p_i = \frac{freq_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}{freq_i}}\]

This is easily accomplished with a single line of R code:

mdist$p<-mdist$freq/sum(mdist$freq)

Calculating the correlation of Runs and Wins

LN 10 - RVs that Vary Together showed how to calculate the correlation of two random variables. This correlation is different than the correlation you may be used to calculating using Excel’s or R’s built-in functions. These functions calculate sample correlations and assume that every outcome occurs with equal probability. It turns out this is true for the Mariners joint distribution, but it will not be true for every joint distribution. Therefore, it is best to calculate the correlation correctly using probability theory.

We start by calculating the covariance:

\[COV(X,Y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}{Pr\{X=x \; \cap \; Y=Y\}} \cdot (x_i - \mu_X)(y_i - \mu_Y) = p_i \cdot (x_i - \mu_X)(y_i - \mu_Y)\] where

\[\mu_X = E[X] = \sum_{i=1}^{n}Pr\{X=x_i\} \cdot x_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i x_i\] \[\mu_Y = E[Y] = \sum_{i=1}^{n}Pr\{Y=y_i\} \cdot y_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i y_i\] Then we calculate the correlation:

\[COR(X,Y) = \frac{COV(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \cdot \sigma_Y}\] where

\[\sigma_X = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i \cdot(x_i - \mu_X)^2}\]

\[\sigma_Y = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i \cdot(y_i - \mu_Y)^2}\] All of this is much easier to calculate in R than you’d expect:

# calculate Runs and Wins means
muR<-mdist$R %*% mdist$p 
muW<-mdist$W %*% mdist$p

# calculate Runs and Wins standard Deviations
sigmaR<-sqrt(mdist$p %*% (mdist$R - as.vector(muR))^2)
sigmaW<-sqrt(mdist$p %*% (mdist$W - as.vector(muW))^2)

# covariance of Runs and Wins
covRW<-mdist$p %*% ((mdist$R - as.vector(muR))*(mdist$W - as.vector(muW)))

# correlation of Runs and Wins
corRW<-covRW / (sigmaR * sigmaW)

corRW
##           [,1]
## [1,] 0.7569778

The correlation of Mariner Runs and Wins is nearly 0.76 percent. This isn’t really a particularly surprising outcome. Intuition would suggest that runs and wins are correlated.

Independence

Recall from the Laws of probability that two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent iff:

\[Pr\{A \; \cap \; B\} = Pr\{A\}Pr\{B\}\] Let’s see how to do this with a joint distribution we construct. This time, we’ll use all of the data for the American and National Leagues instead of just the Seattle Mariners. The steps in the code below are almost identical to the work we did for the Mariners. Interpretting the Correlation

The correlation has a very precise interpretation:

A positive correlation indicates the extent to which one RV is above its mean while the second RV is also above its mean. A negative correlation indicates the extent to which one RV is below its mean while the second RV is above its mean.

There really is no additional meaning we can attribute to a correlation. We know that the correlation, \(r\)

  • can assume values \(-1 \leq r \leq 1\)
  • values of \(|r|\) near 0 indicate little or no correlation
  • values of \(|r|\) near 1 indicate a strong correlation.

A Common Misconception to Avoid

I frequently hear people describe the correlation incorrectly, e.g. “a positive correlation indicates that when one variable is increasing, the other variable is increasing”. THIS INTEPRETATION IS INCORRECT. Unfortunately, I hear even faculty teaching courses state this intepretation (probably because they were taught incorrectly). A look at the formula for calculating the correlation should prove this to you (but I’ll do more in just a moment). Note there is nothing in the correlation formula that deals with direction or movement, only deviations from the mean. Unless we calculate directional differences in each variable’s data and evaluate the relationship between those, we can conclude nothing about the nature of the change relationship.

The following graphs show two random variables.

The graphs shows the values of \(X\) (left) and \(Y\) (right) and the directional change (U or D) is the plot character for the point. For example, a U indicates that the current point moved up from the previous point (and a D indicates the point moved down from the previous point). Every value of \(Y\) represents upward movement from the previous value and every value of \(X\) but one similarly shows upward movement relative to the previous point.

If the correlation somehow reflects when two random variables are moving in the same direction (or the opposite direction for a negative correlation), then these two graphs would clearly show two positively correlated random variables.

The calculated correlation for these two random variables is -0.5714286. Not only is this not a positive correlation, but it’s a moderately strong negative correlation. Adding the means of \(X\) and \(Y\) to the graphs will shed some light on what is happening.

Now we can clearly see that in almost every case in which \(X\) is above its mean, \(Y\) is below its own mean (and vice versa). In other words, our definition for correlation expressed exactly what we see here. Correlation is strictly related to how the random variables behave relative to their means; the changing direction interpretation is clearly wrong.

The following example is even more interesting.

x<-c(seq(200,155,-5), seq(100,145,5))
#y<-c(seq(10,14.5,.5), seq(15.5,20,.5))
y<-c(seq(15.5,20,.5), seq(14.5,10,-.5))
n<-length(x)
xmu<-mean(x)
ymu<-mean(y)

cor(x,y)
## [1] 0.5714286
xpch<-c("-",sapply(2:n, function(i){ifelse(x[i] > x[i-1], "U", "D")}))
ypch<-c("-",sapply(2:n, function(i){ifelse(y[i] > y[i-1], "U", "D")}))

par(mfrow=c(1,2))
plot(x, pch=xpch,type="o")
lines(1:20,rep(xmu,n))
plot(y, pch=ypch,type="o")
lines(1:20,rep(ymu, n))

par(mfrow=c(1,1))

Based on these new graphs and the incorrect interpretation of the correlation, we would expect the two random variables to be nearly perfectly negatively correlated. Whenever \(X\) is decreasing, \(Y\) is increasing, and whenever \(X\) is increasing, \(Y\) is decreasing. However, the calculated correlation is actually 0.5714286. The two RVs are positively correlated!

The moral of this story… evaluate the correlation exactly according to the definition and forget anything you may have previously heard about its meaning.

# beer and diapers example

# read data (include only columns 1 through 4)

dat<-read.csv("Grocery item sales example.csv")[,1:4]

# assign "nice" column names
colnames(dat)<-c("obs", "p", "Diapers", "Beer")

# add the probabilities
dat$p<-rep(1/31, length(dat$p))

# calculate the RV means
mubeer<-dat$p %*% dat$Beer
mudiapers<-dat$p %*% dat$Diapers

# calculate the RV standard deviations
sbeer<-sqrt(dat$p %*% (dat$Beer - as.vector(mubeer))^2)
sdiapers<-sqrt(dat$p %*% (dat$Diapers - as.vector(mudiapers))^2)

# calculate covariance and correlation
covxy<-dat$p %*% ((dat$Beer-as.vector(mubeer))*
                    (dat$Diapers-as.vector(mudiapers)))

r<-covxy/(sbeer * sdiapers)
r
##           [,1]
## [1,] 0.4134689
